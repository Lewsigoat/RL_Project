# VIST (Vista Energy) — Audio Overview Script

## 1. Cold open

This is an overview of Vista Energy, which trades on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker V-I-S-T. Everything here is as of the close on Friday, August fourteenth, twenty twenty-six. This is educational, not investment advice, and oil equities move fast, so treat every number as a starting point for your own checking, not a conclusion.

Here is the short version. Vista is a pure-play bet on one shale field, in one country, at a moment when the oil price is being held up by a war. It is genuinely one of the lowest-cost oil producers on earth, it is growing production faster than almost any listed producer, and it trades at roughly seven times forward earnings. All three of those things are true at the same time, and the reason they are all true at the same time is Argentina, and the Strait of Hormuz. Understanding those two things is most of the work.

## 2. What the company actually is

Vista Energy is legally a Mexican company — a Sociedad Anónima Bursátil, registered in Mexico. But almost all of its operations are in Argentina. That mismatch is a historical artifact. The company was founded in twenty seventeen by Miguel Galuccio, who previously ran Y-P-F, Argentina's state oil company, and before that was a senior executive at Schlumberger. He raised money through a Mexican listed vehicle and used it to buy Argentine assets.

So today you have Class A shares listed on the Mexican stock exchange under the ticker VISTA, and American Depositary Shares listed on the New York Stock Exchange under V-I-S-T, which listed in July twenty nineteen. When you buy V-I-S-T you are buying a depositary receipt over a Mexican holding company whose assets are Argentine oil wells. That is three jurisdictions in one line item, and it matters for how you think about risk, taxes and disclosure.

On disclosure specifically: Vista is a foreign private issuer. It files a twenty-F annual report, not a ten-K, and it does not file quarterly ten-Qs. You get less frequent, less standardized reporting than you would from a comparable U.S. producer.

The asset is Vaca Muerta. That is a shale formation in the Neuquén basin in western Argentina — it is one of the largest shale resources outside North America, and it is the reason anyone cares about Argentine oil. Vista has been drilling there since twenty seventeen and has put roughly seven billion dollars into the ground. It is now the largest crude oil exporter in Argentina, and it exports around seventy percent of what it produces.

## 3. The operating numbers

In the second quarter of twenty twenty-six, Vista produced about one hundred and fifty-six thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day. That is up thirty-two percent from a year earlier. Oil specifically was about one hundred and thirty-five thousand barrels a day, up thirty-three percent. This is a company that is compounding production at a rate you almost never see in a producer of this size.

For the full year twenty twenty-six, guidance is one hundred and fifty-eight thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day, with the third quarter around one hundred and sixty thousand and the fourth quarter around one hundred and seventy thousand. Management has said it is targeting roughly two hundred and fifty thousand barrels a day by twenty thirty.

The single most impressive number in the whole company is the lifting cost: four dollars and fifty cents per barrel of oil equivalent, and falling. That is world-class. For context, most North American shale operators are several times that. Broader all-in costs including depreciation but excluding royalties have run around thirty-three dollars a barrel, which implies a Brent breakeven somewhere below fifty dollars. That low-cost position is the real moat here, and it is what lets the company survive scenarios that would kill higher-cost producers.

Growth has not been purely organic. Vista bought Petronas's fifty percent stake in the La Amarga Chica block for around one and a half billion dollars in twenty twenty-five. Then in the second quarter of twenty twenty-six it closed the acquisition of Equinor's Argentine assets — Bandurria Sur and Bajo del Toro. That deal is the main reason the year-over-year numbers look so dramatic, and it is also the main reason the balance sheet has more debt on it than it used to.

## 4. The financial numbers

Second quarter twenty twenty-six revenue was one point one five billion dollars, up eighty-nine percent year over year. Adjusted EBITDA was eight hundred and five million dollars, up ninety-nine percent. Net income was three hundred and twenty-two million dollars, up thirty-seven percent.

Now here is a wrinkle worth pausing on. Despite those headline numbers, adjusted earnings per share came in at two dollars and thirty-eight cents against a consensus estimate of three dollars and fifteen. That is a miss of about twenty-four percent. So the operational story and the reported-earnings story diverged. When you see a company grow EBITDA by ninety-nine percent and still miss on earnings per share, the gap is usually sitting in financing costs, depreciation from the acquired assets, share count, or currency and tax effects. If you are doing real work on this name, go read the actual filing and find out which — because that gap is the difference between a great quarter and a merely good one.

On cash: free cash flow excluding merger and acquisition activity was four hundred and ninety-one million dollars for the quarter. Capital expenditure was four hundred and ninety-four million. Look at those two numbers next to each other. Vista is roughly reinvesting its entire operating cash flow back into the ground. That is normal for a company in a growth phase, but it means the free cash flow available to shareholders today is close to zero. You are not being paid to wait. There is no dividend. There have been small share buybacks executed on the Mexican exchange.

On the balance sheet: gross debt was three point six six billion dollars at quarter end, against cash of six hundred and five million. So net debt of roughly three billion. Net leverage was one point four one times adjusted EBITDA, or one point two five times on a pro forma basis that accounts for a full quarter of the acquired assets. Management's stated goal is to get net leverage down to around one times by the end of twenty twenty-six.

At eighty-five dollar oil, that leverage is comfortable. At fifty-five dollar oil, it is not comfortable at all. Hold that thought.

## 5. Valuation and where the stock sits

The stock closed Friday at about sixty-eight dollars. That gives a market capitalization of roughly seven point six billion dollars on about one hundred and eleven million shares outstanding. Add net debt of about three billion and you get an enterprise value in the region of ten and a half to eleven billion dollars.

Management guides to three billion dollars of adjusted EBITDA for twenty twenty-six at eighty-five dollar oil. So the enterprise value to EBITDA multiple is somewhere in the mid three times range. Trailing price to earnings is about nine. Forward price to earnings is about seven.

Those are deep value multiples for a company growing production over thirty percent a year with four-dollar-fifty lifting costs. Which should immediately make you ask the right question, which is not "why is it so cheap" but "what is the market pricing in that I might be ignoring." The answer is a country risk discount and an oil price that the market does not believe is sustainable. We will get to both.

On the price action: the fifty-two week range is thirty-one dollars and sixty-three cents at the low to eighty-one dollars and forty-four cents at the high. So the stock has more than doubled off its low, and it currently sits roughly sixteen percent below its high, which it made around June. Year to date it is up about forty-one percent. Realized and implied volatility are both running around forty-three percent annualized, which is high — you should expect this to move a lot in both directions.

One data note: some screeners report a negative beta for this stock, around minus zero point four six. Do not take that literally as a hedge against the market. It is an artifact of Argentina-specific and oil-specific moves that happened to run opposite to the S and P over the measurement window. This is not a defensive holding.

Sell-side sentiment is strongly positive. Consensus rating is around Strong Buy across roughly twelve analysts, with average price targets in the ninety to ninety-eight dollar range. J.P. Morgan has an Overweight rating with an eighty-nine dollar target. Bear in mind that sell-side targets on high-beta commodity names tend to follow the commodity, and they get revised fast.

There was also a notable news item on Friday, August fifteenth: a regulatory filing disclosed that Thiel Macro, Peter Thiel's investment vehicle, had bought roughly one point two million American Depositary Shares, about seventy-six million dollars' worth, which is around one percent of the company. Reportedly it was the second largest position in that portfolio after Amazon, and the only non-U.S. name. The stock rose about four percent on the news. Treat that as a sentiment data point, not a thesis. Thirteen-F style filings are backward-looking snapshots — they tell you what someone owned at a past date, not what they own now or why.

## 6. Now the risks. This is the important part.

**Risk one, and it is the biggest one: the oil price, and specifically the Strait of Hormuz.**

Brent was trading around eighty-seven dollars a barrel on Friday. That is not a normal eighty-seven dollars. Since early March twenty twenty-six, the Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed to U-S and Israel-allied shipping following the conflict involving Iran. Roughly thirteen million barrels a day normally move through that strait — about a fifth of global oil consumption. Traffic has collapsed to something like eight to fifteen vessels a day against roughly one hundred and thirty before the conflict. Brent spiked above one hundred and forty dollars in the early phase and has since retreated as the market adjusted.

Here is why this dominates everything else. Underneath that geopolitical shock, the physical oil market is structurally oversupplied. OPEC plus has been restoring production, non-OPEC supply has been resilient, and both the I-E-A and OPEC itself have been cutting demand growth forecasts — OPEC has now revised its twenty twenty-six demand growth down four consecutive times, to around five hundred and eighty thousand barrels a day. Some forecasters have Brent falling to the mid fifties if the strait reopens and the glut reasserts itself. Goldman has published a fourth-quarter Brent projection around fifty-four dollars.

So when you buy Vista at sixty-eight dollars, you are, whether you intend to or not, taking a position on the Strait of Hormuz. If it reopens and oil goes to fifty-five, Vista's guidance — which is built on eighty-five dollar oil — does not hold, the three billion dollars of EBITDA does not materialize, and the leverage ratio that looked comfortable at one point four times starts looking like two and a half or three times. The company has explicitly said it would consider cutting spending if its realized crude price falls below fifty-five dollars a barrel. If instead the deadlock drags on or escalates, oil goes higher and Vista is a direct beneficiary. You should be honest with yourself about which of those you are betting on, because the equity is a leveraged expression of that view.

**Risk two: Argentina.**

Vista's assets, revenues, workforce and regulatory exposure are all Argentine. That means currency controls, capital repatriation restrictions, inflation, and the perennial question of whether the government of the day lets exporters keep their dollars.

Right now the setup is about as good as it has ever been. President Milei's government has liberalized, international capital markets have reopened to Argentine energy companies, and the October twenty twenty-five midterm win strengthened the reform agenda. There is a scheme called R-I-G-I — a large investment incentive regime created in twenty twenty-four offering tax breaks, customs rebates and favourable exchange treatment locked in for thirty years. Vista has joined it. That thirty-year stability guarantee is real and meaningful.

But export duties still exist and, by the company's own framing, add something like thirty percent to costs. Management has been lobbying for their removal and describes elimination as "the icing on the cake" — meaning it is upside, not something you should assume. And Argentine political cycles have reversed direction before, repeatedly, within investors' living memory. A thirty-year contractual stability regime is worth a great deal right up until a future government decides to test it. That is the country risk discount you are being paid to accept.

**Risk three: the price Vista actually receives.**

Argentine export crude — the Medanito grade — has historically realized around eighty percent of the Brent price. So headline Brent overstates what lands in Vista's revenue line. Vista realized eighty-nine dollars and forty cents a barrel last quarter, up forty-four percent year over year, which was excellent, but that was under wartime pricing conditions. Model the discount, not the headline.

**Risk four: the shale treadmill.**

Shale wells decline steeply — often fifty to seventy percent in the first year. Vista's production growth is a function of continuously drilling new wells. Stop spending and production falls quickly. This is why capex is running at roughly the same level as free cash flow. It also means the company's ability to grow is directly tied to its ability to keep funding drilling — which ties back to oil prices and to access to capital markets, which ties back to Argentina.

**Risk five: infrastructure and takeaway capacity.**

Growing to two hundred and fifty thousand barrels a day is meaningless without a way to move the oil. Vista holds a stake in V-M-O-S, a shale oil pipeline and export port project. It is currently around sixty-five percent complete with expected completion in mid twenty twenty-seven. The long-term growth case depends on that project landing roughly on time and on budget. Large energy infrastructure projects in emerging markets have a well-documented tendency to do neither.

**Risk six: concentration.**

One basin. One country. One commodity. There is essentially no diversification inside this company. Every risk above is correlated with every other risk above. When Argentina has a bad week, all of them fire at once.

**Risk seven: structural and governance items.**

You own an A-D-S over a Mexican holding company. Founder-led with a concentrated influential shareholder base. Reduced-frequency reporting as a foreign private issuer. No dividend. Currency translation running through Mexican pesos, Argentine pesos and U-S dollars. None of these are disqualifying, but they are all friction, and they all belong in your position sizing.

## 7. What to watch from here

Six things.

One, the Strait of Hormuz. This is the single largest driver of the next twelve months and it is entirely exogenous to the company.

Two, third quarter results, due around November. The specific test is whether they hit the roughly one hundred and sixty thousand barrel a day target, and whether earnings per share converges back toward what EBITDA implies after the second quarter miss.

Three, deleveraging. Management said net leverage around one times by year end twenty twenty-six. That is a checkable, dated commitment. Hold them to it.

Four, Argentine export duties. Removal is genuine upside that is not in most models. An increase would be a serious negative.

Five, V-M-O-S pipeline progress toward mid twenty twenty-seven.

Six, realized price relative to Brent — whether that eighty percent relationship holds.

## 8. How to think about it

Let me try to be genuinely useful rather than balanced-sounding.

The bull case is not complicated and it is not stupid. This is a top-decile-cost producer growing production more than thirty percent a year, trading around seven times forward earnings and mid-three times EBITDA, in a country that has just undergone real and durable-looking economic liberalization, with a thirty-year investment stability regime, and with a large resource base that supports the growth trajectory for years. If oil holds anywhere near current levels and Argentina stays on its current path, this is cheap and the analyst targets in the nineties are not fantasy.

The bear case is equally simple. The current oil price is a war premium sitting on top of a structurally oversupplied market. Strip the premium and you get Brent in the fifties, Medanito in the forties, guidance that misses badly, leverage that goes the wrong direction, and a country with a long history of changing the rules on exporters when it needs dollars. In that scenario this stock does not fall twenty percent, it falls a lot more — remember it traded at thirty-one dollars within the last fifty-two weeks.

Both of those are coherent. What that tells you is that this is not a name where the analysis resolves into a clean answer — it is a name where you have to take a view on an exogenous geopolitical variable, and then size the position for the possibility that you are wrong about it. It is a high-volatility, high-conviction-required, single-country commodity equity. It is not a core holding and it is not a substitute for diversified energy exposure.

The practical implications. Size it as a satellite position, not a core one — the volatility alone argues for that. Recognise that you are underwriting an oil price view, so form that view deliberately rather than inheriting it. If you cannot articulate what you think happens to Brent when Hormuz reopens, you do not yet have a thesis, you have a valuation multiple. Read the twenty-F rather than relying on screeners, because the legal structure, the currency exposure and the tax treatment are all in there and are not in the summary data. And decide in advance what would make you change your mind — a specific oil price, a specific Argentine policy change, a specific missed production number — because in a name this volatile, deciding that in the middle of a drawdown does not go well.

To close with the honest summary: Vista is a very good operator holding a genuinely world-class asset, priced at a discount that is mostly rational rather than mostly a mistake. The discount exists because of two risks that are real, large, and outside management's control. Whether it is a buy depends almost entirely on how you handicap those two risks, and that is a judgement about geopolitics and Argentine politics, not a judgement about oil wells.
