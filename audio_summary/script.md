# Nineteen Eighty-Four — Audio Summary, Part One, Chapters 1 to 6

## [INTRO]

Nineteen Eighty-Four, by George Orwell, published in nineteen forty-nine. This is a summary of Part One, chapters one through six. These opening chapters do not move the plot very far. What they do instead is build the world, and show us a man who has begun, very quietly, to break.

## [CHAPTER 1]

Chapter One.

It is a bright, cold day in April, and the clocks are striking thirteen. Winston Smith, thirty-nine years old, with a varicose ulcer above his right ankle, climbs seven flights of stairs to his flat in Victory Mansions. The lift rarely works. Electricity is cut during daylight hours as part of an economy drive for Hate Week.

On every landing there is a poster of an enormous face, a man of about forty-five with a heavy black moustache. The caption reads: Big Brother is watching you.

Inside his flat there is a telescreen. It transmits and it receives at the same time. It cannot be switched off, only dimmed. Any sound Winston makes above a low whisper will be picked up. Any movement he makes within its field of vision can be seen. Nobody knows how often, or on what system, the Thought Police plug in on any individual wire. You simply have to live as though every sound is overheard and every movement scrutinized.

Orwell then lays out the world. London is the chief city of Airstrip One, itself the third most populous province of Oceania. Above the city rise four vast pyramids of white concrete, the four ministries that make up the entire apparatus of government. The Ministry of Truth, which handles news, entertainment, education and the arts. The Ministry of Peace, which handles war. The Ministry of Love, which maintains law and order. And the Ministry of Plenty, which is responsible for economic affairs. In Newspeak, the official language, they are Minitrue, Minipax, Miniluv and Miniplenty.

On the white face of the Ministry of Truth, where Winston works, are the three slogans of the Party: War is peace. Freedom is slavery. Ignorance is strength.

Winston has done something dangerous. He has bought a diary, a beautiful cream-papered book from a junk shop in a slum quarter of the city, and he has bought a pen to write in it. Nothing is illegal, because there are no longer any laws. But if he is caught, it is reasonably certain he will be punished by death, or by twenty-five years in a forced labour camp.

He sits in a shallow alcove beside the window, the one spot in the room where the telescreen cannot see him, and he begins to write. He puts down the date: April the fourth, nineteen eighty-four. Then he stops, because he cannot actually be certain of the year, or of his own age. He is writing for the future, for the unborn, and he does not know whether the future will be able to hear him.

His first entries are disordered. He describes a war film he saw the night before, an audience laughing at footage of refugees being bombed. Then he describes that morning's Two Minutes Hate.

The Hate is a daily ritual. On the telescreen appears the face of Emmanuel Goldstein, the Enemy of the People, a former leading Party member turned traitor, the supposed author of every heresy and every act of sabotage. Within thirty seconds the room is shouting. People leap in their chairs, scream, throw things at the screen. The horrible thing about the Hate, Winston reflects, is not that you are obliged to act a part, but that it is impossible to avoid joining in. The rage is an abstract, undirected emotion that can be switched from one object to another like the flame of a blowlamp.

During the Hate, Winston notices two people. One is a girl of about twenty-six with dark hair, who works in the Fiction Department, wearing the scarlet sash of the Junior Anti-Sex League. Winston hates her on sight, precisely because she seems the most orthodox person in the room. The other is O'Brien, a large, burly member of the Inner Party. For one instant their eyes meet, and Winston becomes convinced, with no evidence at all, that O'Brien is politically unorthodox, and that they are on the same side.

The Hate ends with the chant of B-B, B-B, over and over. Back at his desk afterwards, Winston finds he has written, in large capitals, filling half a page: Down with Big Brother. Again and again.

He has committed thoughtcrime. The Thought Police, he understands, will get him sooner or later. Then there is a knock at the door.

## [CHAPTER 2]

Chapter Two.

The knock is only his neighbour, Mrs Parsons, a woman of about thirty who looks fifty, asking him to unblock her kitchen sink because her husband is out. Winston goes.

The flat is squalid, and her children are appalling. A boy of nine and a girl of seven, dressed in the uniform of the Spies, prance around him shouting traitor and thought-criminal, and the boy fires a catapult at the back of his neck. Nearly all children now are like this, Winston reflects. The Party has systematically turned them into ungovernable little savages, and yet it produces no impulse to rebel against the Party at all. The opposite. The devotion is to the Party, and adults are routinely denounced by their own children.

Back in his flat, Winston remembers a dream from seven years earlier, in which a voice in the darkness said to him: We shall meet in the place where there is no darkness. He believes the voice was O'Brien's.

He writes in the diary again, and reaches the thought that clarifies everything for him: thoughtcrime does not entail death. Thoughtcrime is death. He is already a dead man. The only question is how long he can postpone the arrest.

He puts the diary away, and carefully places a single whitish speck of dust on the cover, so that he will know if anyone has moved it.

## [CHAPTER 3]

Chapter Three.

Winston wakes from dreams. In one, his mother and his young sister are sinking in the saloon of a ship, somewhere far below him, looking up at him through darkening water. He knows, obscurely, that they died so that he could live, and that such a death is no longer possible in the present age, because tragedy belonged to the old time, a time of privacy, love and friendship.

In another dream he is in a sunlit landscape he calls the Golden Country, and the dark-haired girl walks toward him and tears off her clothes with a single magnificent gesture, a gesture that seems to annihilate an entire culture, an entire system of thought. He wakes with the word Shakespeare on his lips.

Then the telescreen shrieks, and it is time for the Physical Jerks, morning exercises led by a barking instructress.

While he bends and stretches, Winston tries to think about the past, and finds that he cannot verify any of it. He has no clear memories before the mid nineteen fifties. There are no external records. Every book, every newspaper, every photograph has been altered. Oceania is at war with Eastasia and allied with Eurasia, and has always been so. Yet Winston distinctly remembers that only four years ago it was exactly the reverse.

This is the Party's central claim, and its central slogan: Who controls the past controls the future. Who controls the present controls the past.

And here Orwell introduces the key mental technique of the regime: doublethink. The power to hold two contradictory beliefs in one's mind simultaneously, and accept both of them. To tell deliberate lies while genuinely believing them. To forget any fact that has become inconvenient, and then, when it is needed again, to draw it back from oblivion for just so long as it is needed. And, crucially, to apply the same process to the process itself: to be unconscious of the act of hypnosis you have just performed. Even to understand the word doublethink involves the use of doublethink.

His reverie is broken by the instructress screaming at him through the telescreen. Smith. Six thousand and seventy-nine, Smith W. Yes, you. Bend lower, please. Even in a room of hundreds, he is being watched individually.

## [CHAPTER 4]

Chapter Four.

We see Winston at work. His job at the Ministry of Truth is the continuous rectification of the past.

At his desk are three orifices: one for incoming instructions, one for newspapers, and one slot for waste paper, known as the memory hole. Anything dropped into a memory hole is carried away to enormous furnaces. Winston receives small slips of paper listing errors in back issues of The Times, and he corrects them.

The word error is a euphemism. What has happened is that reality has changed, and the record must be brought into line. Big Brother promised in December that there would be no reduction of the chocolate ration. In April the ration is reduced from thirty grammes to twenty. So the December speech is rewritten to contain a warning that a reduction would probably be necessary in April. A prediction about the war on the South Indian front turned out wrong, so the prediction is replaced with one that came true. When the corrected copy is filed and the original burned, the falsified version becomes the truth. No other evidence ever exists.

Winston's most interesting assignment that day concerns a certain Comrade Withers, once a prominent Inner Party member, who has been purged and vaporized. He is an unperson. He never existed. But Big Brother devoted a speech to praising him, and that speech is in the record.

So Winston invents a replacement. He creates, out of nothing at all, Comrade Ogilvy: a boy who joined the Spies at six, denounced his uncle to the Thought Police at eleven, invented a hand grenade, took a vow of celibacy, and died heroically in action at twenty-three. Comrade Ogilvy never lived. But now, in the record, he exists as certainly as any man who ever did, and Winston takes a genuine craftsman's pleasure in the forgery.

## [CHAPTER 5]

Chapter Five.

Lunch in the Ministry canteen: watery stew, gritty bread, victory gin that smells like nitric acid. Winston sits with Syme, a philologist working on the Eleventh Edition of the Newspeak Dictionary.

Syme is enthusiastic, and what he says is the ideological heart of the book. Newspeak is the only language in the world whose vocabulary gets smaller every year. The destruction of words, he explains, is a beautiful thing. Why keep bad when you have ungood. Why keep excellent and splendid when you have plusgood and doubleplusgood.

Then he says it plainly. The whole aim of Newspeak is to narrow the range of thought. In the end, thoughtcrime will be literally impossible, because there will be no words in which to express it. Orthodoxy means not thinking. Orthodoxy is unconsciousness.

Winston listens and thinks, with certainty: Syme will be vaporized. He is too intelligent. He sees too clearly and speaks too plainly. The Party does not like such people.

They are joined by Parsons, Winston's sweaty, cheerfully stupid neighbour, collecting subscriptions for Hate Week. At a nearby table a man is quacking rapid orthodoxy at a companion without appearing to think at all — a sound Newspeak has a word for: duckspeak, which is praise when applied to someone you agree with.

Then the telescreen announces a triumph. The chocolate ration has been increased to twenty grammes a week. The crowd cheers. Yesterday it was thirty, and it has been cut. Winston knows this, because he personally rewrote the record that morning. And yet everyone around him genuinely believes the increase, including, perhaps, Parsons. This is doublethink in action, at scale.

Looking up, Winston catches the dark-haired girl from the Fiction Department watching him. She looks away instantly. He is convinced she is following him, that she is an amateur spy, and that she may already know what he is thinking. He is badly frightened.

## [CHAPTER 6]

Chapter Six.

Winston writes in the diary about something that happened three years earlier: an encounter with a prole prostitute in a dark basement. He had gone with her deliberately, wanting to commit a crime, and in the room's light he saw that she was about fifty, painted, and toothless. The memory disgusts him and he sets it down anyway, because the diary is a confession.

This leads him to the Party's treatment of sex, which is the chapter's real subject. The aim is not merely to prevent men and women from forming loyalties the Party cannot control, but to remove all pleasure from the sexual act. Desire is thoughtcrime. There is the Junior Anti-Sex League, advocating complete celibacy for both sexes and the production of children by artificial insemination. Marriage must be approved by a committee, and permission is refused if the couple appear to be physically attracted to one another. The only recognized purpose is to beget children for the service of the Party.

Winston remembers his own marriage, to a woman named Katharine, tall, fair-haired, and utterly, rigidly orthodox — a woman with, as he puts it, the most stupid and vulgar mind he had ever encountered, and not a thought in her head that was not a Party slogan. She was frigid, and she called their weekly attempt our duty to the Party, and she insisted on it as a duty even though she disliked it. They stayed together about fifteen months and then separated. Divorce is not permitted, but separation is encouraged when there are no children.

And Winston understands the mechanism at last. Sexual privation is not a side effect of the regime. It is a tool. Frustrated desire produces hysteria, and hysteria can be converted into war fever and leader worship. The Party takes an ordinary human drive, dams it up, and burns it as fuel.

He ends the entry frustrated. He wants not just to record, but to be heard. And he is writing to a future that may not exist, in a language that is being deliberately dismantled.

## [OUTRO]

That is Part One, chapters one through six.

To pull the threads together: Orwell spends these chapters establishing four instruments of control. Surveillance, in the telescreen and the informant children. The manipulation of history, in Winston's own job. The mutilation of language, in Newspeak. And the manipulation of private feeling, in the Party's war on sex and family.

Against all of that stands one man with an illegal notebook, who already knows he is going to be caught, and writes anyway. The famous line from the diary sums up his position, and the book's: freedom is the freedom to say that two plus two make four. If that is granted, all else follows.
