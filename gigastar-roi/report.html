How this was built, and what it can't tell you

  • The universe. SEC EDGAR full-text search returns 45 GigaStar "Channel Drop" issuers that have filed a Form C, matching the platform's own count of 45 offers. Six never funded — Jonathan DesVerney Gospel Music 1, The Shluv Family 2, Ace1Whips 1, Mr_Organik 1, Sebastine 1 and Flix Media 1 — so the buyable basket is 39.
  • The Gold tier is the lowest tier. Each Form C states that a Gold CRT is 1 revenue-sharing unit, Platinum 4, and Diamond 16, and that "each tier has the same economic value per dollar amount invested." Buying the lowest tier therefore costs the least and changes nothing about the rate of return.
  • Return per unit. Because distributions are strictly pro-rata per unit and tier pricing is linear, a drop's return per Gold CRT equals its total distributions divided by its total raise. That derivation reproduces GigaStar's own published per-unit figures exactly on 10 of the 11 drops where it publishes them.
  • One disagreement. Let's Talk Money 3 is the exception: GigaStar reports $2.47 per unit where the drop's own economics give $4.93, a clean 2× gap that looks like the March 2026 restatement of CRTs into standardised Gold units. The drop's own arithmetic is used here; the alternative would cut the basket's return from 14.73% to 14.65%.
  • Fees are excluded. GigaStar currently charges $5 per order plus $3 for ACH. Thirty-nine separate small orders would add roughly $312, pulling the basket return from 14.73% down to about 13.4%. Historical fee schedules may have differed, so this is a sensitivity, not a restatement.
  • Payback, extrapolated. The fifteen drops at least 24 months old have returned 32.3%, an average of 12.6% a year. Held flat, that implies roughly an eight-year payback before any profit — and YouTube revenue decays, so flat is optimistic.
  • Reconciliation. Per-drop distributions sum to $1,448,236 against the $1,478,489 on GigaStar's headline counter, a 2% gap consistent with the two endpoints refreshing on different schedules.
  • Not advice. These are illiquid, speculative securities. Past distributions do not predict future ones, and a secondary market that would let you exit was still not trading as of this date.
